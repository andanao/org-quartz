This book was simultaneusly page-turning forget what time it is fascinating, and take a surprised nap boring. Parts of it were incredibly interesting, to me, in particular the chapter relating to emotions and expressiveness when related to L1 and L2 expressions. However other sections were significantly more drab and academic that I didn’t enjoy nearly as much as I found the emotional section of the book.

The author, I think quite understandably, has a fascination with Nabakov and Chagall, and keeps bringing up their writings throughout the book, and I learned more about them and their lives through reading this book than I ever had in the rest of my life. I get why though, they were people who’s primary emotional language that allowed them to express themselves was Russian, and while completely fluent in the languages in which they did most of their work (english and french) they always felt that Russian was their home language. I can totally see why Pavlenko loves this and can relate, having grown up speaking Russian and Ukranian.

I really wish I could find the article that I heard about this book from because it would be interesting to compare to, but I don’t even know where to start searching for it because it was never saved anywhere and I read it all the way back when I was at AILA.

Overall I really liked Pavlenko’s view on the Sapir Whorf hypothesis (as reformulated by Lennenberg and Brown) that language influences thought. She basically says that this is a bullshit thing to be asking about since what the hell is that question even trying to ask, while it may seem easy to understand at first, the reality is that the question is incredibly loaded and has a lot of context to it, and what is language in the brain even? Plus the supposed ‘whorfian’ observations made about colors, emotions, movement, really only serve to justify a western viewpoint of the subject and we classify things based on things that we think are entirely natural based off of our experiences, but that many cultures have never had a need to call seperate things. Things like numbers, describing the color of something, saying what time something will happen, that we find so incredibly important, aren’t even always relevant to certain groups: why the hell would a hunter gatherer have the need to count to 103,213? Or describe the exact shade of putrid brown on a peice of paper? Its irrelevant to their lives, and it really reflects how much language is a tool that evolves to suit the needs of the people using it.

The book really made me realize not only how broad the languages of the world are, but how even the things that we think of as universal i.e. color, numbers, time, movement, emotions, are cultural phenomenon that we don’t even realize that we have been given. Another point is the sheer number of differences in languages that exist, particularly among hunter gatherer and small tribes, they have such complex languages with such a wide variation in them that boggles the mind.

Additionally this book has also driven home for me a couple of things: the incredibly pressing need today to try and document the worlds languages that exist as best as possible, languages are dying because their speakers no longer think that they are relevant to their lives or helpful, so they are understandably not using them or not teaching them to their children, but if we want to be able to understand what I belive to be one of humanities greatest evolutionary adaptations and a window into the different ways in which the mind works, there is a need to document them while they are still around and used as the primary language by their speakers.

One of the last things that I have to say about this book is that through reading this and noticing the insane amount of questionable methodological practices employed through the countless studies cited in this book: I really distrust social science, perhaps more than before. It is next to impossible to control for all variables, and sample sizes are basically 0, and researchers tend to simply ingore effects that they think would make their research uninteresting or complicate it. I don’t think I can trust the results of a study if within 10 seconds of reading about what the study is and looking at the methodology realizing that there are some major issues with how the research is being conducted. I understand that money is tight and that things are far more difficult in social science to get relevant data, but that doesn’t mean that you can just claim whatever you want.

In all this book did an excellent job highlighting some of the problems in research and congnition related to bilingualism, and the issues that working from a monolingual framework has created. I enjoyed reading much of it, although not all of it. I’m not sure that I know anyone who I would reccomend read this book off of the top of my head, although Dr. Ramsey did come to mind various times throughout the reading of this book, and I would really love to know his thoughts on the topic.

Look up the Plains Indian Sign Language Lingua Franca system

While this is fascinating, all the talk of methodological issues in experiments reminds me why I don’t trust results/conclusions from social science beyond the ability to do a job stamp collecting.

Its amazing how little I cared about grammar froms and structure when I was learning about it in school, but now I’m reading about it for fun and finding it absolutely fascinating.

Interesting views on the armchari linguists of Chomsky and Pinker and insistance on the focus of learning the actual languages instead and leaving the ivory tower to go get your hands dirty. They rely too much on the questionable tales of linguistic anthropology (where it takes 6 months to become “fluent” and “you don’t need to be able to construct a sentance in a language to have a deep understanding of it”) and making assumptions based on that, but I do think it is important to develop a theoretical framework of how language works in the brain. However the armchair hypothesizing is a lot sexier than going to go do fieldwork somewhere in bumfuck nowhere with none of the furnishings of modern life.
