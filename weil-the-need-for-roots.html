The Need for Roots

  • philosophy
  • book

Sep 06, 2026

Related: The Human Condition Leisure: The Basis of Culture The Philosophical Act Simone Weil

How can a country go about rebuilding a society totally destroyed by war? Moreover what if that society had become morally bankrupt and ceased believe it’s own purpose and became Nazi conspirators? Simone Weil attempts to tackle this thorny topic writing for the Free French out of London.

Simone Weil wasn’t convinced by the idea of human rights, the idea of rights requires a legal system to recognize it, she believed that there are obligations preceede rights, in her words:

#+begin_quote The notion of obligations comes before that of rights, which is subordinate and relative to the former. A right is not effectual by itself, but only in relation to the obligation to which it corresponds, the effective exercise of a right springing not from the individual who possesses it, but from other men who consider themselves as being under a certain obligation towards him. Recognition of an obligation makes it effectual. An obligation which goes unrecognized by anybody loses none of the full force of its existence. A right which goes unrecognized by anybody is not worth very much. #+end_quote pg. 3

Needs of the Soul

Individually we have obligations to meet in order to survive: breathe, drink, eat, sleep etc. Likewise we also have social obligations and an obligation towards other people. While the body can be deprived of these things, human life is much more than that:

#+begin_quote Every one knows that there are forms of cruelty which can injure a man’s life without injuring his body. They are such as deprive him of a certain form of food necessary to the life of the soul. #+end_quote pg. 6

Weil identifies the needs of the soul to be:

  • Order
  • Liberty
  • Obedience
  • Responsibility
  • Equality
  • Hierarchism
  • Honour
  • Punishment
  • Freedom of Opinion
  • Security
  • Risk
  • Private Property
  • Collective Property
  • Truth
  • Rootedness

That many of these needs directly contradict eachother comes from our need to balance what we need a balance between all of these. The immediate question following this is what happens to an individual and to society at large when the needs of the soul are not met or fall completely out of balance?

[Up]Rootedness

#+begin_quote To be rooted is perhaps the most important and least recognized need of the human soul. It is one of the hardest to define. A human being has roots by virtue of his real, active and natural participation in the life of a community which preserves in living shape certain particular treasures of the past and certain particular expectations for the future #+end_quote pg. 40

In short to be rooted is to be part of a community, in France at the time the traditional forms of community had largely been uprooted through the industrialization. However it wasn’t only the working class who moved to cities that were uprooted, it was also the farmers who were uprooted while not having left. This uprootedness in her view left people ripe to jump on to any sort of cause to believe in.

It’s clear that her life experiences shaped these views, from her time spent in Germany studying the rise of Nazism despite having what she and many in the rest of Europe believed to be the best educated and most receptive population to the realizations of the power of the proletariat, these well educated people all fell in line behind Hitler. Similarly in France when the control of the country folded as the country was given over to the Nazis and a Nazi puppet state.

Likewise her experience with the uprootedness in the cities and towns came from her year working in automotive factories around Paris and her final post as a schoolteacher. As a laborer it was clear to her the dehumanizing realities of factory work, where the most important thing is meeting production quotas and the first thing to go in order to feed the beast is your mind, as one exhausts themselves working on small parts whose purpose in some greater machine is invisible to the individual factory worker.

The source of uprootedness in the countryside is quite different, but seems to be similarly influenced by her time as a schoolteacher in rural areas and working as a farm hand throughout her life and in the summers (in large part to experience the life of the rural poor). To a her the reason is not the work, but rather being forgotten by the nation, seen as backward, and put in contrast to the urban proletariat.

Lastly there is the uprootedness of the french nation itself, which she blames on the ineptitude of the 3rd republic to represent it’s people. In Weil’s view a democracy requires an engaged and educated populous to work, people who care not only about the wellbeing and future of the nation but also respect and understand the nation for what it is. It sticks out to me that to her the proof of this uprootedness in the nation is visible in how poorly politians were viewed in France at the time: as power hungry buffoons and how the government is viewed as a joke and not worthy of respect. Which is ridiculous since a democracy is supposed to represent the will of the people (and yet here we are in 2023…) and they should feel ownership and pride over their government and nation.

The Growing of Roots

So how to fix this uprootedness? To Weil who spent her life immersed in the politics attempting to bring about reform, the solution moved away from political and towards the relegious. It’s also where I tend to disagree with her point and tend towards a less literal reading of the relegious emphasis (extremely catholic despite her secular jewish upbringing). In her view the re growing of roots involves fostering community, creating work that is fufilling (often manual labor) and a spiritual connection to God.

Through capitalism and industrialization society was left aimless in its pursuits with individual desires instead left to reign free without any purpose or direction, easily coopted by charismatic and visionary leaders. In this process she notes that science was replacing relegion despite few being able to understand its minutea, and fewer still capable of contributing, creating an environment in which we all recieve our beliefs passed down through interpretations and abstractions of texts that have been detatched from the real world (These views on science is more striking given her relationship to her brother Andre Weil, one of the most influential mathematicians of the 20th century). She comes to the conclusion that science (and modern philosophy) cannot answer our basic questions of why are we here, what are we doing and where shall we go as a society, and tha only relegion can provide those answers.

Conclusion and general thoughts

It’s been a theme of my recent reading thinking about society and how there needs to be a reckoning about the limits of science to reshape society. In no small part this thinking has been set off by the very visible and rapid progress of AI systems. At this point we are a society which has defined ones based on job: pick a random person and ask them who they are, and instead you will be told what they do. It is precicesly this society that also dreams of having AI free us from our labor. At the same time we no longer have beleive in our ability to reshape society, now all that seems to happen is arguments over a marginal tax changes. How is such a society supposed to confront an existential threat to the core of our identiy when we it seems that we can’t even agree is brown people should be treated like humans in a notianally democratic country?

Weil’s answer is clear, community, manual labor, and God; which while interesting to in some ways ask to turn back the clock, there is merit to realizing the importance of our work and labor, we still need science, but maybe there is room for spirituality and relegion in the 21st century. After all, it’s relegion that has historically provided a way to understand life, society and the future, but also provide guidance and a community to changing world. Unfortunately the last point has been forgotten and now relegion seems to only be about clinging to outdated dogmatic beliefs and a tool for justifying ones own racism/mysogyny/homophobia and so on rather than trying to be a way to collectively understand the world.

Comparisons of Weil and Arendt

While reading I kept comparing Weils proposal about how Arendt tackled similar issues in The Human Condition yet her conclusions are entirely secular, proposing elevating Action to a higher status in our society while also questioning not the purpose of science, but what direction it serves. To both Weil and Arendt science only provides the means, but the judgments of value and worth should not come from the scientists themselves but rather from society.

Aside from the future of society, they both were quite focused on actual practical matters, Weil working for the french resistance and fighting in Catalonia in the 30s, and Arendt focus on humanitarian concerns and political matters rather than ivory tower philosophy.
